\begin{abstract}
A world model plans with its predictions, but the world it is deployed in contains disturbances the model never saw.
\emph{Hindsight planning} conditions the plan on what actually happened: it reads the surprise between predicted and
observed outcomes, infers the hidden cause, and re-plans. We ask when this is provably and measurably better than
planning forward with the model alone. Theory gives a sharp answer: a Bayes hindsight planner is never worse; it is
strictly better only when the disturbance \emph{persists}, where its regret grows logarithmically against linearly for
forward planning; it gains nothing when disturbances are fresh; and the popular residual-reuse rule is inconsistent for
sequential re-planning. Experiments on a physics simulator and on real measured systems confirm each prediction and add
a second condition the theory implies: hindsight matters when control is \emph{open-loop}. With every-step feedback its
gain is \ksGainOne; with 40-step open-loop chunks it is \ksGainForty{} (oracle headroom \ksHeadForty). All hypotheses
were pre-registered, including those that failed, and we report the failures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Latent world models now plan by rolling predictions forward and optimising actions against them
\citep{cwm_2611_24778,adajepa_2606_32026,t2z_2610_22364}. When deployment differs from training (a gust, a weak
actuator, a miscalibrated gain) predictions drift and plans fail. Three families of fixes have appeared: reuse the
last observed residual \citep{cwm_2611_24778}, optimise an environment latent at test time \citep{t2z_2610_22364}, or
update the model's weights online \citep{adajepa_2606_32026}. All three condition on observed outcomes; we call this
\emph{hindsight planning}. None of these papers proves when it beats forward planning.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) A unified view: forward planning, residual reuse and latent inference are one Kalman-style filter at gain $0$,
gain $1$ and the Bayes-optimal gain (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
(2) Five theorems: dominance, no gain under fresh disturbances, linear-vs-logarithmic regret under persistence,
inconsistency of residual reuse, and a condition for hierarchical planners (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
(3) Pre-registered experiments on real measured systems and an interactive MuJoCo benchmark showing where the gains
appear, how large they are, and where they vanish (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).
(4) Negative results: hindsight did not rescue hierarchical planning in our river-crossing task, and an autoresearch
loop selected an estimator that failed on sealed test data (Section~\ref{sec:neg}).

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}
A frozen world model $f$ predicts the next state. The world adds a hidden disturbance $\phi_t$: a gain fault $\theta$
that scales the effect of the action, an additive force $w$, or both. A \emph{forward} planner ignores outcomes. A
\emph{hindsight} planner maintains a belief over $\phi$ from the residuals $r_t=\Delta s_t-f(s_t,a_t)$ and plans under
it. With $\phi_t$ a random walk of variance $q$ per step, the Bayes belief is a Kalman filter whose gain runs from $0$
(constant $\phi$, gain $\to1/t$, recursive least squares) to $1$ as $q\to\infty$, which is exactly residual reuse. Forward
planning is gain $0$; residual reuse is gain $1$; test-time latent inference with a window or forgetting factor sits in
between.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
\paragraph{Protocol.} Every experiment has a pre-registration committed before it ran
(\texttt{papers/latent-hindsight-planning/*\_preregistration.md}), fixed analysis rules, provenance-stamped results
(commit, clean tree, seeds), and an \emph{oracle gate}: the planner that knows the true disturbance must beat forward
planning, otherwise the benchmark is not measuring what we think. The gate failed twice; both failures and their
diagnoses are reported in the pre-registration files.

\subsection{Real measured systems}
Five benchmarks from nonlinearbenchmark.org \citep{nonlinear_benchmarks}; official train/test splits; NARX world models
trained on train only; all hyper-parameters fixed before any test data was read. One benchmark (EMPS) is excluded from
every claim because model-free velocity extrapolation beats every learned model on it. Over \rdRecords{} test records,
the gain/offset posterior with forgetting reduces error relative to forward prediction to \rdOneStep{} (one step) and
\rdTenStep{} (ten steps); residual reuse is \emph{worse} than forward at ten steps (\rdResidTen). Online last-layer
updates \citep{adajepa_2606_32026} are stronger at one step (\rdSgdOne). In a sealed test, a hybrid (online updates plus
posterior) reaches \rdHybridFwd{} of forward error at ten steps, better than forward in \rdHybridSigFwd{} seed-records, but
equals online updates alone (\rdHybridSgd; significantly worse in \rdHybridWorseSgd). On these systems the inputs are
logged, so no planner chooses actions, and the residuals left after online adaptation are close to fresh, the case where
Theorem~\ref{thm:fresh} predicts no further gain.

\subsection{Interactive physics: MuJoCo}
A puck driven by two motors (MuJoCo \citep{todorov2012mujoco}) with a hidden actuator gain and an external force; learned
5-model ensemble world models with a disagreement penalty \citep{chua2018pets}; CEM-MPC executing open-loop chunks of
$K=20$ steps; the puck is reset at each goal segment while the disturbance and each planner's memory persist. Methods:
forward; residual reuse; online last-layer SGD; Gaussian gain/offset posterior; latent inference by gradient descent
(T2-Z style) or by particles; hybrid; and an oracle given the true disturbance. 3 seeds $\times$ 20 episodes per regime.

\paragraph{How open-loop is control? (Table~\ref{tab:k}, Figure~\ref{fig:sweeps}a.)} With every-step re-planning the
oracle's headroom is near zero and hindsight's gain is \ksGainOne; with $K=40$ it is \ksGainForty{} against a headroom of
\ksHeadForty. Feedback from re-planning absorbs most disturbances; hindsight matters when the agent commits to actions.

\paragraph{How noisy is the world? (Table~\ref{tab:noise}.)} Adding i.i.d.\ gusts on top of the persistent wind,
residual reuse degrades from \nzResidZero{} to \nzResidOne{}, as Theorem~\ref{thm:residual} predicts. The Gaussian

(\nzAdaptZero{} to \nzAdaptOne). Hindsight estimators must be calibrated to deployment noise, not training residuals.

\paragraph{What is identified?}\label{sec:ident} After 20 transitions the inferred wind explains \idWindTwenty{} of its
variance across episodes (Figure~\ref{fig:sweeps}c), while the gain is poorly identified. The inferred parameters also
drop at every chunk boundary. This is not a defect: the data identify $\theta a+w$, not $\theta$ and $w$ separately,
and each new action rotates that ridge. Pinning the gain removes the drop (boundary dip \cfDipStd{} $\to$ \cfDipPin;
final $R^2$ \cfRPin), and the identifiable quantity, the predicted effect of the next action, \emph{improves} at
boundaries (dip \idPredDip) and reaches $R^2=$\idPredEnd. Interpretability claims about inferred latents should be made
about identifiable combinations.

\section{Negative results}
\label{sec:neg}
\paragraph{Hierarchical planning (river crossing).} We tested whether hindsight rescues a hierarchical planner whose
subgoal fails because of a surprise (Theorem~\ref{thm:hier}). Across three pre-registered designs it did not.
v1: an abort-and-retreat rule made hindsight worse (success \rvOneHind{} vs \rvOneFwd{} forward; flat planners
\rvOneFlat). v2: the current was identified one step after entry, but no plan in a fixed library was safe (success
\rvTwoHind{} vs \rvTwoFwd; oracle \rvTwoOracle). v3: with MPC re-planning every step, forward planning pushed through
(\rvThreeFwd) and the oracle's bridge execution failed (\rvThreeOracle), so the main run was not executed. In every case
inference worked and execution, or fast feedback, decided the outcome. We therefore do not claim that hindsight beats
hierarchical planning; we claim the narrower Theorem~\ref{thm:hier}, whose conditions these tasks rarely met.
\paragraph{Autoresearch.} An automated keep-or-discard loop over estimators, scored on development data and one-step
error, selected an estimator that was worse than forward at ten steps on sealed test data. The metric, not the search,
was at fault; we report the sealed result rather than the development score.

\section{Limitations}
One simulated physical system and five real benchmarks with logged inputs; learned dynamics are small MLP ensembles on
low-dimensional state, not pixel JEPAs; gain faults are poorly identified without deliberate excitation; the
hierarchical claim is untested in a task where its conditions hold.
